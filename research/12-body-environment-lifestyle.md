# 12 · Body, environment & lifestyle

## Overview

This area collects explanations that cultural debates about modern misery tend to skip: how people sleep, move, eat, drink and medicate; how much daylight and darkness they get; what they breathe; where they grow up; and what happens inside bodies that are lonely, inflamed, heavier or reaching puberty earlier. These are "proximal" factors. They sit close to the nervous system, and many can be measured and some can be manipulated in trials. That gives this area something the others mostly lack: a handful of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and Mendelian randomisation (MR) studies that test causal direction instead of only describing correlations. Throughout, entries separate three kinds of evidence. Observational evidence is cross-sectional or prospective cohort work. Experimental evidence comes from RCTs. Quasi-causal genetic evidence comes from MR, which uses inherited gene variants as a natural experiment, though it has its own assumptions, notably no pleiotropy.

The evidence is uneven. A few findings are robust across designs. Insomnia predicts later depression, and treating insomnia reduces depressive symptoms in trials. Physical activity is associated with lower incident depression in prospective cohorts and in MR analyses of accelerometer-measured activity. Higher BMI appears to raise depression risk in MR. Urban upbringing is strongly linked to schizophrenia. Many other findings are associations, at best prospective, with plausible confounding and reverse causation. These include ultra-processed food, green space, light at night, air pollution, the gut microbiome and hormonal contraception. Several popular stories turn out weaker than their fame. Bright light at bedtime from phones has small measured effects on adolescent sleep in some of the best data. The "sleep recession" is clear for US adolescents but much less clear for adults. Large prevention trials of nutritional supplements have been null or even slightly adverse.

There are three main camps. The first is the **proximal-causes camp**: sleep, activity, light, diet and toxins plausibly account for a meaningful share of depressive and anxious symptoms. On this view, fixing them is cheap, concrete and testable, and grand cultural theories may be unnecessary for much of the variance. The second is the **social-determinants camp**, which overlaps with areas 02 and 05. It accepts the bodily mechanisms but treats them as downstream of how societies are organised: sedentary indoor work, long commutes, food environments, screen-centred leisure, housing near traffic, and loneliness built into how people live. The third is the **deflationary or sceptical camp**. Its members point out that many lifestyle trends do not line up in time with rising distress, and that some cut the wrong way: youth drinking has fallen, adult sleep may not have shortened, and lead exposure has dropped steeply. They add that effect sizes are mostly small and that healthy-user and reverse-causation biases inflate observational estimates.

The main fault lines are these:
- **Timing.** Does the factor change in the right direction, in the right groups, at the right time to explain rising distress?
- **Causality.** Is the factor a cause, a consequence or a marker of depression? Depression itself disrupts sleep, appetite, activity and social contact.
- **Effect size versus population impact.** A small individual effect spread across a whole population can still matter.
- **Responsibility.** Lifestyle framing can shift attention from collective conditions to individual "choices". Area 11 covers this.

The area cross-cuts the rest of the project. Inflammation is treated mechanistically in area 03. Screens and sleep overlap with area 06. Deaths of despair, including opioid deaths, belong to area 02. Heat and suicide belong to area 09. Mismatch arguments about indoor, sedentary, isolated life overlap with area 05.

### Secular trends in sleep duration ("the great sleep recession")
- **id:** sleep-duration-secular-trends
- **type:** debate
- **disciplines:** sleep science; epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, time-acceleration, measurement-diagnosis
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Katherine Keyes and colleagues (adolescent decline); Lisa Matricciani, Tim Olds (sceptical reviews of the claim, though one of their own analyses found a decline); Yu Sun Bin; Shawn Youngstedt (adult sceptics)
- **core claim:** A common story says modern life, with its 24-hour economy, artificial light and screens, has steadily eroded sleep, and that chronic sleep loss feeds mood problems. The evidence splits by age and method. Self-reported adolescent sleep in the US clearly declined from the 1990s. Long-run data on children show a decline in pooled estimates, but with big regional variation and a weak citation base behind the popular claim. Adult sleep, especially when measured objectively, shows little or no secular decline.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Keyes et al. analysed Monitoring the Future, a yearly nationally representative US survey of adolescents from 1991 to 2012 (N = 272,077). Self-reported sleep "generally declined over 20 years". The largest change came between 1991–95 and 1996–2000, and age-period-cohort models showed a period effect concentrated in the late 1990s and early 2000s [S1]. The authors noted that part of this decline predates smartphones. Press coverage reported that just over half of 15-year-olds got at least 7 hours in 1991, versus under 43% in 2012 [S1, figure as reported by press coverage of the paper].
  - Matricciani, Olds & Petkov pooled data on 690,747 children aged 5–18 from 20 countries, dating from 1905 to 2008. The sample-weighted median rate of change was −0.75 minutes per night per year, "a decrease of more than 1 h per night over the study period" [S3].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - The same pooled analysis found that sleep *increased* in Australia, the UK and Scandinavia, while it fell in Europe overall, the USA, Canada and Asia [S3]. So the direction depends on country.
  - Matricciani, Olds & Williams traced the *claim* of declining child sleep. Of 51 papers, 34 asserted a decline without presenting data. The 17 that presented data were mixed: 2 increases, 3 no change, 6 mixed and 6 decreases [S2].
  - Bin, Marshall & Glozier examined 38 time-use surveys of adults in 10 industrialised countries (n = 328,018), from the 1970s to the 2000s. Short sleep (≤6 h) increased in Italy and Norway but *decreased* in Sweden, the UK and the US. Long sleep became more common in several countries [S5].
  - Youngstedt et al. reviewed 168 studies of objectively measured sleep in healthy adults (polysomnography or actigraphy; 6,052 people, 1960–2013). They found "no significant association of sleep duration with study year" [S6].
  - A narrative review from the Matricciani group discusses the empirical basis for secular-trend claims and their public-health implications [S4]. Only its abstract was read.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. The adolescent decline in self-reported US data is solid, but child trends vary by country and objectively measured adult sleep shows no clear decline.
- **related:** screens-bedtime-sleep, insomnia-predicts-depression, adolescent-mental-health-trends (area 04), haidt-great-rewiring (area 06)
- **quote (optional):** "Limited increases in short sleep duration challenge the claim of increasingly sleep-deprived societies." — Bin, Marshall & Glozier, *Am J Epidemiol* 2013, abstract [S5]

### Insomnia as a prospective risk factor for depression
- **id:** insomnia-predicts-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** sleep medicine; psychiatric epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Chiara Baglioni, Dieter Riemann and colleagues
- **core claim:** People who are not depressed but have insomnia are roughly twice as likely to develop depression later. On this view insomnia is not merely a symptom of depression but precedes it, which makes it a candidate target for prevention.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Baglioni et al. meta-analysed 21 longitudinal epidemiological studies published 1980–2010. The random-effects odds ratio for baseline insomnia predicting later depression was 2.60 (95% CI 1.98–3.42). After outliers were handled, the fixed-effects OR was 2.10 (1.86–2.38) [S7].
  - The temporal ordering supports the idea that insomnia comes first, at least in part. The trial evidence below strengthens the causal reading [S8, S9].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - The authors themselves flag the main limit: included studies "did not always consider the role of other intervening variables" [S7]. Stress, pain, anxiety and prodromal depression could all drive both insomnia and later depression.
  - Insomnia is one of the diagnostic criteria for depression. Early or subthreshold depression can show up first as poor sleep, which would produce a spurious "predictor".
  - Even if the effect is causal, it does not show that insomnia *rose* in the population in step with depression. See sleep-duration-secular-trends.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. The prospective association is consistent across 21 studies in meta-analysis. The causal share remains uncertain from these data alone.
- **related:** treating-insomnia-reduces-depression, sleep-duration-secular-trends, stress-diathesis-model (area 03)

### Treating insomnia reduces depressive symptoms (OASIS and meta-analysis)
- **id:** treating-insomnia-reduces-depression
- **type:** intervention
- **disciplines:** clinical psychology; sleep medicine
- **themes:** body-biology, treatment-help
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Daniel Freeman (OASIS); Alexander Scott, Thomas Webb (meta-analysis)
- **core claim:** If poor sleep causes mental-health problems, improving sleep should reduce them. Large trials of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), including digital versions, and a meta-analysis of 65 trials find that improving sleep reduces depression, anxiety and rumination. Larger sleep gains produce larger mental-health gains.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - OASIS was a single-blind RCT at 26 UK universities, in which 3,755 students with insomnia were randomised to digital CBT-I or usual care. At 10 weeks, insomnia fell substantially (Cohen's d = 1.11). The prespecified primary mental-health outcomes, paranoia (d = 0.19) and hallucinations (d = 0.24), also fell, with insomnia mediating the change [S8].
  - In OASIS's secondary outcomes at 10 weeks, depression (PHQ-9) fell by an adjusted −2.83 points (d = 0.48). Anxiety (GAD-7) fell by −1.86 (d = 0.33), and wellbeing (WEMWBS) rose by 2.47 (d = 0.29), all p < 0.0001 [S8, Table 4 of full text].
  - Scott et al. meta-analysed 65 RCTs (72 interventions; N = 8,608) in which sleep was improved. Pooled effects were g = −0.63 for depression, −0.51 for anxiety, −0.49 for rumination and −0.53 for composite mental health. Greater improvements in sleep quality were associated with greater mental-health improvement [S9].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - OASIS had heavy attrition. The authors describe dropout as high, about 50%, and greater in the treatment arm: only about 39% of the treatment arm and 61% of controls provided week-10 data (calculated from counts in the paper) [S8]. Differential dropout can bias estimates.
  - Participants in CBT-I trials cannot be blinded, and the mental-health outcomes are self-reported. Expectancy and demand effects therefore cannot be excluded. This problem recurs across lifestyle trials (see exercise-and-depression, diet-intervention-trials).
  - OASIS studied university students. Its authors note that generalisation "requires testing" [S8].
  - Showing that treating insomnia helps does not show that population-level sleep has worsened enough to explain trends in distress.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. A very large RCT plus a 65-trial meta-analysis with a dose-response pattern, although every trial was unblinded and self-report based.
- **related:** insomnia-predicts-depression, exercise-and-depression, behavioural-activation (area 03)

### Screens, bedtime devices and sleep
- **id:** screens-bedtime-sleep
- **type:** debate
- **disciplines:** sleep science; developmental psychology; media studies
- **themes:** technology-attention, body-biology
- **layer:** technology
- **proponents:** Lauren Hale, Ben Carter (association reviews); Amy Orben, Andrew Przybylski (sceptical re-analysis)
- **core claim:** Screen use, especially devices in the bedroom at night, is widely blamed for adolescent sleep loss through displacement, arousal and light. Reviews of mostly cross-sectional studies find consistent associations. Analyses using better measurement, such as time-use diaries, find the associations small.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Hale & Guan reviewed 67 studies (1999–2014) and found that screen time was adversely associated with sleep, mainly shorter duration and later timing, in 90% of them [S10].
  - Carter et al. meta-analysed 20 cross-sectional studies (125,198 children; mean age 14.5). Bedtime use of portable devices was associated with inadequate sleep quantity (OR 2.17, 95% CI 1.42–3.32), poor sleep quality (OR 1.46) and daytime sleepiness (OR 2.72). Merely having access to a device at night was also associated with worse outcomes [S11].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Hale & Guan list key limitations themselves: causal association not confirmed, measurement error in both screen time and sleep, and little data on multi-screen use [S10].
  - Carter et al.'s included studies were all cross-sectional and relied on self-report [S11].
  - Orben & Przybylski analysed 11,884 UK Millennium Cohort adolescents and compared retrospective self-report with time-use diaries. They found "a small negative association" between digital engagement and sleep (median standardised β = −0.06 on weekdays, −0.03 on weekends). Contrary to their own expectations, technology use before bedtime was not substantively associated with how much adolescents slept [S12].
  - Keyes et al.'s adolescent sleep decline began in the late 1990s, before smartphones were widespread [S1]. That timing suggests other drivers as well.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. The direction of association is consistent, but effects shrink with better measurement, and causal direction is not established.
- **related:** sleep-duration-secular-trends, haidt-great-rewiring (area 06), orben-przybylski-specification-curve (area 06), indoor-life-light-circadian

### Physical activity, inactivity and depression (trends, cohorts, MR and treatment)
- **id:** exercise-and-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** exercise science; psychiatric epidemiology; genetic epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, treatment-help, work-economy
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Felipe Schuch, Brendon Stubbs (cohort meta-analysis); Matthew Pearce, James Woodcock (dose-response); Karmel Choi, Jordan Smoller (MR); Michael Noetel (treatment network meta-analysis)
- **core claim:** Physical activity is associated with lower risk of developing depression across ages and regions, with the steepest benefit at low doses. MR using accelerometer-measured activity suggests the protective direction is at least partly causal. As treatment, exercise produces moderate symptom reductions in trials. Activity levels in high-income countries are low and, on WHO's pooled estimates, insufficient activity increased between 2001 and 2016.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - *Trends.* WHO's pooled analysis of 358 surveys (1.9 million people) found global insufficient activity stable at about 27.5–28.5% from 2001 to 2016. In high-income countries it was 36.8% in 2016, more than double low-income countries' 16.2%, and it rose over the period (about 32% to 37% in summaries of the paper). Women in high-income Western countries stood at 42.3% [S13]. Among school-going adolescents aged 11–17 worldwide, 81% were insufficiently active in 2016. Boys improved slightly and girls did not [S14].
  - *Prospective cohorts.* Schuch et al. pooled 49 prospective studies (N = 266,939; 1.84 million person-years). High versus low activity was associated with lower odds of incident depression (adjusted OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.79–0.88), consistently in youths (0.90), adults (0.78) and older people (0.79) [S15].
  - *Dose-response.* Pearce et al. pooled 15 cohorts (191,130 adults). Compared with no activity, half the recommended volume (4.4 mMET-h/week) carried 18% lower depression risk and the recommended 8.8 mMET-h/week carried 25% lower risk, with diminishing returns beyond that. They estimate that 11.5% of cases could have been prevented if less-active adults had met recommendations [S16].
  - *Mendelian randomisation.* Choi et al. used two-sample MR with GWAS data on 611,583 adults of European ancestry. Genetically predicted accelerometer-measured activity was associated with lower odds of major depression (OR 0.74 per 1-SD increase in mean acceleration, 95% CI 0.59–0.92). There was no evidence for the reverse direction, from MDD to activity [S17].
  - *Treatment* (see area 03). Noetel et al.'s network meta-analysis of 218 RCTs (14,170 participants with major depression) found moderate effects versus active controls for walking or jogging (g = −0.62), yoga (−0.55), strength training (−0.49) and mixed aerobic exercise (−0.43) [S18].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Schuch et al. found significant publication bias, though adjustment did not change the estimates [S15]. Pearce et al. report large heterogeneity (I² = 74%) [S16].
  - In Choi et al., *self-reported* activity showed no significant MR relationship with MDD, and only the accelerometer instrument did [S17]. The MR rests on a single accelerometer GWAS from UK Biobank, and pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded.
  - The Cochrane review (39 trials) found a pooled SMD of −0.62. When restricted to the six trials with adequate allocation concealment, intention-to-treat analysis and blinded assessment, the effect was small and non-significant (SMD −0.18, 95% CI −0.47 to 0.11) [S19]. Noetel et al. likewise found that only one included study met Cochrane criteria for low risk of bias, and rated confidence as low to very low [S18].
  - Global insufficient activity was flat from 2001 to 2016 [S13]. The trend data are self-reported and do not obviously track the timing of rising youth distress in every country.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. The protective association is replicated across cohorts, dose-response and MR. Treatment effects are real but inflated by low-quality trials.
- **related:** passive-vs-active-sedentary, treating-insomnia-reduces-depression, exercise-as-treatment (area 03), evolutionary-mismatch (area 05)

### Sedentary behaviour: passive versus mentally active, and screen sitting
- **id:** passive-vs-active-sedentary
- **type:** concept
- **disciplines:** exercise science; epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, technology-attention
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Mats Hallgren, David Dunstan, Neville Owen; Aaron Kandola, Joseph Hayes
- **core claim:** Sitting is not one thing. Passive sedentary time, such as television viewing, may relate to depression differently from mentally active sedentary time, such as reading or some computer use. In adolescents, objectively measured sedentary time that rises across the teenage years predicts higher depression scores at 18, and light activity predicts lower scores.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Kandola et al. studied 4,257 ALSPAC adolescents with accelerometry at 12, 14 and 16. Sedentary time rose from about 431 to 523 minutes a day and light activity fell from about 326 to 245 minutes a day across those years. Each extra 60 minutes a day of sedentary behaviour at age 12 was associated with an 11% higher depression score at 18 (IRR 1.111). Each extra 60 minutes of *light* activity was associated with about 10% lower scores (IRR 0.904) [S20].
  - Hallgren, Dunstan & Owen argue that inconsistent sedentary–depression findings may stem from lumping passive and mentally active sitting together. They propose classifying the two separately [S21].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Kandola et al. lost many participants to follow-up: 2,486 had data at 12 and 1,220 at 16 [S20]. Accelerometers capture sitting, not what is done while sitting.
  - The passive/active distinction is a review-level proposal. The authors themselves list measurement, causality and mechanisms as open questions [S21].
  - This literature is observational, and lower mood plausibly reduces activity.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Objective prospective data show associations, but the passive/active framework is still a hypothesis and causality is unestablished.
- **related:** exercise-and-depression, screens-bedtime-sleep, social-media-and-adolescent-depression (area 06)

### Indoor life, daylight deficit and light at night
- **id:** indoor-life-light-circadian
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** chronobiology; environmental epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, time-acceleration
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Sean Cain, Angus Burns (Monash; UK Biobank light studies); Neil Klepeis (exposure science)
- **core claim:** Modern people spend most of their time indoors under dim daytime light and relatively bright evening light, which blurs the day–night signal that entrains the circadian clock. In large UK Biobank analyses, more daytime outdoor light is associated with lower odds of depression and better sleep, and more light at night with higher risk of several psychiatric disorders.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - NHAPS was a 1992–94 US EPA telephone diary survey (n = 9,386). Respondents spent on average 87% of their time in enclosed buildings and about 6% in enclosed vehicles [S22]. The often-quoted "90% indoors" figure appears to round this up.
  - Burns et al. (2021) studied UK Biobank adults aged 37–73, who reported a median 2.5 hours a day outdoors in daylight. Each extra hour outdoors was associated with lower odds of lifetime MDD (OR 95% CI 0.92–0.98), antidepressant use, anhedonia and low mood, and with fewer insomnia symptoms and easier waking, independent of demographic, lifestyle and employment covariates. Longitudinal cross-lagged models pointed the same way with smaller effects [S23].
  - Burns et al. (preprint 2022; reported as published in *Nature Mental Health* 2023) used wrist-worn light sensors for about a week in 86,772 UK Biobank adults with mean age 62. Brighter night-time light was associated with higher risk of MDD, generalised anxiety, PTSD, psychosis, bipolar disorder and self-harm. Independently, brighter daytime light was associated with lower risk of MDD, PTSD, psychosis and self-harm, robust to adjustment for sociodemographics, photoperiod, physical activity and sleep quality [S24]. Press coverage reported roughly a 30% higher depression risk with the brightest night-light exposure and about 20% lower with the brightest days [S24, press-reported figures; not checked against the published tables].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - All of the UK Biobank evidence is observational. People who are depressed may go outside less, keep irregular hours and lie awake with lights or screens on, so reverse causation is plausible.
  - The 2021 outdoor-light measure was self-reported [S23]. The 2022/23 study was cross-sectional, measured light over a single week, and used an older, healthier-than-average volunteer cohort [S24].
  - NHAPS is 1990s US data and does not show a *trend* [S22]. This project found no comparable time series showing indoor time rising in step with distress.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Large, consistent associations with plausible mechanisms, but observational, with obvious reverse-causation risk and no trend data linking them to rising distress.
- **related:** bright-light-therapy, screens-bedtime-sleep, sleep-duration-secular-trends, evolutionary-mismatch (area 05)
- **quote (optional):** "Humans evolved in an environment with a clear distinction between day and night, but our modern environments have blurred this distinction." — Burns et al. 2021, *J Affect Disord*, abstract [S23]

### Bright light therapy and seasonal affective disorder
- **id:** bright-light-therapy
- **type:** intervention
- **disciplines:** psychiatry; chronobiology
- **themes:** body-biology, treatment-help
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Robert Golden and colleagues; Edda Pjrek, Dietmar Winkler and colleagues
- **core claim:** Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) shows that light exposure can drive mood in some people. Bright light boxes (≥1,000–10,000 lux) outperform dim-light or sham controls in RCTs for SAD and, on thinner evidence, for non-seasonal depression.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Golden et al. reviewed RCTs from 1975 to 2003. Bright light for SAD had an effect size of 0.84 (8 studies), dawn simulation 0.73 (5 studies), and bright light for non-seasonal depression 0.53 (3 studies). The authors compared these with antidepressant trial effect sizes [S25].
  - Pjrek et al. meta-analysed 19 blinded RCTs in SAD. Bright light beat placebo (SMD −0.37, 95% CI −0.63 to −0.12; 18 studies, 610 patients), and response was 42% more likely (RR 1.42). They found no evidence of publication bias [S26].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Golden et al. note that only 13% of studies they found met inclusion criteria and that "many reports... are not based on rigorous study designs". Light as an *add-on* to antidepressants showed no effect (ES −0.01) [S25].
  - Pjrek et al.'s more recent estimate is less than half Golden's for SAD. They found moderate heterogeneity and moderate-to-high risk of bias, and call for larger trials [S26].
  - SAD is a minority presentation. The success of light therapy does not show that chronic indoor life explains general rises in distress.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. Several meta-analyses of blinded RCTs agree on benefit for SAD, but the effect is modest in the more recent analysis and the evidence for non-seasonal depression is thin.
- **related:** indoor-life-light-circadian, treating-insomnia-reduces-depression

### Nature exposure and green space
- **id:** nature-green-space
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** environmental psychology; cognitive neuroscience; epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, community-belonging, future-ecology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Gregory Bratman, James Gross; Kristine Engemann, Jens-Christian Svenning; the multi-author ecosystem-services consensus group
- **core claim:** Contact with nature reduces rumination and stress, and growing up with more surrounding greenery is associated with lower risk of later psychiatric disorder. On this view, urban life that cuts people off from nature is part of why modern societies are anxious and depressed.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Bratman et al. (2015) randomised 38 healthy adults to a 90-minute walk in a natural or urban setting near Stanford. Only the nature walk reduced self-reported rumination and neural activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, a region linked to depressive rumination [S27].
  - Engemann et al. (2019) linked satellite vegetation indices around the homes of about 1 million Danes from birth to age 10 with later psychiatric registry diagnoses. Those with the least childhood green space had up to 55% higher risk across various disorders than those with the most. The association persisted after adjustment for urbanisation, socioeconomic factors, parental mental illness and parental age, and cumulative exposure mattered more than any single year [S28].
  - A consensus paper by natural, social and health scientists (Bratman et al. 2019) concluded that nature experience benefits cognitive functioning and emotional wellbeing, and argued for including mental health in ecosystem-service assessments [S29].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Bratman 2015 is small (n = 38), short-term and conducted in healthy young adults, with a single brain region of interest [S27]. It tests acute mood, not depression.
  - Engemann et al. is observational. The press quoted the lead author describing the result as "purely correlational" [S28, as quoted in press coverage]. Greener homes in Denmark likely differ on unmeasured family and neighbourhood factors, and residual confounding by socioeconomic position is a standard critique of green-space epidemiology.
  - "Nature" is defined in many incompatible ways, through vegetation indices, park visits or views. A consensus statement is not an independent replication [S29].
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Experimental effects on rumination are small-sample and short-term, and the large cohort evidence is observational with plausible confounding.
- **related:** urbanicity-and-depression, indoor-life-light-circadian, solastalgia (area 09), rumination-self-focus (area 08)

### Urbanicity: strong for psychosis, weak and mixed for depression
- **id:** urbanicity-and-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** psychiatric epidemiology; social neuroscience
- **themes:** body-biology, community-belonging, status-comparison
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Jaap Peen (meta-analysis); Evangelos Vassos, Robin Murray (schizophrenia); Florian Lederbogen, Andreas Meyer-Lindenberg (neural stress response)
- **core claim:** Wealthy societies are heavily urban, and city life is often blamed for anxiety and depression. The association between urban upbringing and schizophrenia is large and well established. The urban–rural gap for mood and anxiety disorders is much smaller and partly explained by confounders. A brain-imaging study suggests city living and urban upbringing alter neural responses to social stress.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - For schizophrenia, Vassos et al.'s meta-analysis estimated risk in the most urban environments at 2.37 times that in the most rural [S30].
  - For mood disorders, Peen et al. pooled 20 population surveys published since 1985. The urban–rural OR for mood disorders was 1.39 unadjusted (95% CI 1.23–1.58) and 1.28 adjusted (1.13–1.44). For anxiety disorders it was 1.21 unadjusted (1.02–1.42) and 1.13 adjusted (1.00–1.28, p = 0.06). For any psychiatric disorder it was 1.38 unadjusted and 1.21 adjusted, and for substance use disorders the difference was not significant [S31].
  - Lederbogen et al. ran three fMRI experiments with healthy German volunteers. Current city living was associated with greater amygdala activity during a social-evaluative stress task, and urban upbringing with altered perigenual anterior cingulate activity, a region that regulates the amygdala [S32].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - The depression ORs are modest and heterogeneous. Only 33% of mood-disorder comparisons in Peen et al. were individually significant, and the adjusted anxiety OR was borderline [S31].
  - Lederbogen et al.'s samples were small. Exact numbers were not checked here because the full text is paywalled. The study is a single cross-sectional imaging paper and neuroimaging findings of this kind have a mixed replication record. It shows altered stress *processing*, not depression.
  - Urbanicity bundles many exposures: air pollution, noise, less green space, social fragmentation, migration and inequality. "Urban" is a proxy, not a mechanism.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. The depression–urban link is small, partly confounded and inconsistent across studies (the schizophrenia–urban link alone would rate WELL-SUPPORTED).
- **related:** nature-green-space, air-pollution-lead-toxins, old-friends-inflammation, social-capital-decline (area 02), social-defeat-hypothesis (area 03)

### Ultra-processed food and depression (observational)
- **id:** ultra-processed-food-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** nutritional epidemiology; nutritional psychiatry
- **themes:** body-biology, work-economy
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Melissa Lane, Felice Jacka, Wolfgang Marx and colleagues; Chatpol Samuthpongtorn, Raaj Mehta
- **core claim:** Modern diets in wealthy countries are dominated by industrially formulated "ultra-processed" foods (NOVA category 4). Higher intake is associated with higher risk of depression and anxiety in prospective cohorts and cross-sectional surveys, and the associations look dose-dependent.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Lane et al.'s 2024 *BMJ* umbrella review covered 45 pooled analyses (n ≈ 9.9 million). It rated as convincing (class I) the associations of greater ultra-processed food exposure with prevalent anxiety outcomes (OR 1.48) and combined common mental disorder outcomes (OR 1.53). It rated as highly suggestive (class II) the association with incident depressive outcomes (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.16–1.28) [S33].
  - Samuthpongtorn et al. followed about 31,700 US female nurses aged 42–62 in the Nurses' Health Study II. The highest quintile of intake (more than 8.8 servings a day) versus the lowest (fewer than 4) had HR 1.49 (1.26–1.76) for strictly defined depression. A 4-year lag analysis gave HR 1.32, which the authors present as arguing against reverse causation [S34].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - GRADE certainty was *low* for the mental-health associations in the umbrella review, despite the "convincing" classification [S33]. The two scales measure different things: the classification captures statistical consistency, while GRADE captures risk of bias.
  - Reverse causation is a central worry. Depression alters appetite, energy for cooking and food choices. A 4-year lag reduces but does not remove this concern, since depression often has a long prodrome [S34].
  - In Nurses' Health Study II, high consumers had higher BMI, more smoking and more comorbidity and exercised less [S34]. Residual confounding by socioeconomic position and health behaviour is likely.
  - NOVA groups very different products together, from breakfast cereals to soft drinks to processed meats. Its category boundaries are disputed in nutrition science.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. The associations are consistent and dose-dependent but entirely observational, rated low GRADE certainty, and vulnerable to reverse causation and confounding.
- **related:** diet-intervention-trials, obesity-depression, gut-microbiome-depression, old-friends-inflammation

### Diet intervention trials: SMILES, PREDIMED, MoodFOOD and omega-3
- **id:** diet-intervention-trials
- **type:** intervention
- **disciplines:** nutritional psychiatry; clinical trials
- **themes:** body-biology, treatment-help
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Felice Jacka (SMILES); Almudena Sánchez-Villegas, Miguel Martínez-González (PREDIMED); Mariska Bot, Brenda Penninx (MoodFOOD); Olivia Okereke (VITAL-DEP); Katherine Appleton (Cochrane omega-3)
- **core claim:** If diet causes depression, changing diet should treat or prevent it. One small treatment trial (SMILES) reported a very large benefit. A larger cardiovascular trial (PREDIMED) found a non-significant reduction in depression. Large prevention trials of supplements and food-related behavioural therapy (MoodFOOD, VITAL-DEP) were null, or in VITAL-DEP slightly adverse.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - SMILES was a 12-week single-blind RCT of 67 adults with moderate-to-severe depression. Participants got seven dietitian consultations or a social-support control. The diet group improved more on the MADRS (Cohen's d = −1.16), and remission was 32.3% versus 8.0% (NNT 4.1) [S35].
  - Jacka et al. respond that dietary change correlated strongly with symptom change. They cite broad replication, naming the larger HELFIMED trial (n = 152) in their reply, which was not checked here [S37].
  - PREDIMED randomised older Spanish adults at high cardiovascular risk. The Mediterranean diet with nuts versus a low-fat control gave an HR for incident depression of 0.78 (95% CI 0.55–1.10), not significant. In participants with type 2 diabetes it was 0.59 (0.36–0.98) [S38].
  - The Cochrane review of omega-3 for major depression (34 placebo-controlled trials, 1,924 participants) found SMD −0.40, a small-to-modest benefit [S41].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Molendijk, Fried & Van der Does argue that SMILES's "very large effect size" likely reflects "selectively induced expectancy and a loss of blinding", and that recruitment practices were undisclosed [S36]. A correction to the paper added that a website was used in the final year of recruitment [S35, correction]. With 67 participants, of whom 31 and 25 had complete data, a d above 1 is fragile.
  - PREDIMED's main 2013 publication was retracted and republished in 2018 because of randomisation irregularities. These included household members enrolled without randomisation and whole clinics allocated at one site, affecting about 1,588 of 7,447 participants [S39, secondary]. Whether the depression sub-analysis was re-examined was not established here. Its diabetes-subgroup result is a post-hoc subgroup finding.
  - MoodFOOD was a 2×2 factorial RCT of 1,025 overweight or obese adults with subsyndromal depression in four European countries. Neither multinutrient supplements (omega-3, selenium, folic acid, vitamin D) nor food-related behavioural activation therapy prevented major depressive episodes over a year: OR 1.06 for supplements and 0.93 for therapy [S40].
  - VITAL-DEP randomised 18,353 US adults with mean age 67.5 to 1 g/day of marine omega-3 or placebo for a median 5.3 years. Depression risk was slightly *higher* with omega-3 (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.01–1.26), with no difference in mood scores [S42].
  - The Cochrane authors judged the omega-3 treatment effect "unlikely to be clinically meaningful" and the evidence very-low certainty [S41].
- **strength:** CONTESTED. One small, contested positive treatment trial against large null or adverse prevention trials. Diet-as-treatment is unproven, and supplement-based prevention is contradicted by the best trials.
- **related:** ultra-processed-food-depression, exercise-and-depression, obesity-depression

### Youth drinking has declined (a counter-trend)
- **id:** youth-drinking-decline
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** alcohol epidemiology; public health
- **themes:** body-biology, self-identity
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** John Holmes, Michael Livingston, Amy Pennay; HBSC network (Emilia Leal-López, Jo Inchley)
- **core claim:** Alcohol use and alcohol use disorder are linked to depression, plausibly with alcohol driving depression more than the reverse. But adolescent drinking has *fallen* across most high-income countries over the period when youth distress rose. A "more self-medication with alcohol" story cannot explain the youth trend and is undercut by it.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Holmes et al. state that "youth drinking has declined across most high-income countries in the last 20 years", call the decline well established and unlikely to reverse, and say lighter drinking appears to carry into adulthood in some countries [S43].
  - In HBSC data on 250,161 15-year-olds in 39 European and North American countries and regions from 2002 to 2014, weekly drinking, drunkenness and early initiation generally decreased, though not in every country [S44].
  - Boden & Fergusson's review found that alcohol use disorder and major depression each roughly double the odds of the other (pooled adjusted ORs 2.00–2.09). The most plausible causal direction is alcohol use disorder increasing depression risk [S45].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Trends are not uniform. Some countries did not decline or rose on some measures [S44], and declines have slowed or stabilised recently [S43].
  - Falling youth drinking could itself be a *marker* of the same changes some link to distress: less in-person socialising and more time at home and online. This is an interpretive possibility raised in public debate; it was not tested here.
  - Adult drinking trends and harms are a different story. Holmes et al. note that heavier-drinking older generations moving through high-risk ages may keep population harms up [S43].
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. The decline in youth drinking is documented across multinational surveys, which means youth alcohol cannot explain rising youth distress.
- **related:** cannabis-potency-psychosis-depression, deaths-of-despair (area 02), loneliness-trends (area 02), adolescent-mental-health-trends (area 04)

### Cannabis potency, psychosis and depression (and a pointer on opioids)
- **id:** cannabis-potency-psychosis-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** addiction psychiatry; psychiatric epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Marta Di Forti, Robin Murray; Tom Freeman; Gabriella Gobbi
- **core claim:** As alcohol fell among youth, the cannabis market shifted towards higher-THC products. Daily use of high-potency cannabis is associated with markedly higher odds of psychotic disorder and may explain part of the geographic variation in psychosis incidence. Adolescent cannabis use is associated with modestly higher later depression and suicidality.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Freeman et al. meta-analysed 12 studies from 7 countries covering 1970–2017. THC in herbal cannabis rose by about 0.29 percentage points a year, resin rose faster, and CBD stayed stable. The herbal rise was driven by high-THC sinsemilla's growing market share [S46].
  - EU-GEI was a case-control study of 901 first-episode psychosis patients and 1,237 controls across 11 sites. Daily cannabis use was associated with OR 3.2 for psychotic disorder, and daily use of high-potency cannabis with OR 4.8. Removing high-potency cannabis would hypothetically prevent 12.2% of cases across sites, 30.3% in London and 50.3% in Amsterdam [S47].
  - Gobbi et al. pooled 11 prospective studies (23,317 people). Adolescent cannabis use was associated with later depression (OR 1.37, 95% CI 1.16–1.62), suicidal ideation (OR 1.50) and suicide attempt (OR 3.46). The anxiety association (OR 1.18) was not significant [S48].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - EU-GEI is case-control, so it is vulnerable to recall bias and to confounding by other risk factors, and its population attributable fractions assume causality [S47].
  - Gobbi et al. call individual-level risk "moderate to low" and ask for confirmation in adequately powered prospective studies [S48]. Shared vulnerability, such as childhood adversity or early conduct problems, could drive both cannabis use and depression.
  - Potency data come from seizures and market samples, which may not reflect what users actually consume [S46].
  - **Opioids:** the US opioid crisis matters for deaths of despair, but its causes, including supply, prescribing and the illicit fentanyl market, are covered in area 02. No opioid data were verified for this file.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Potency trends and the psychosis association are fairly robust, but the cannabis–depression link is modest, observational and plausibly confounded.
- **related:** youth-drinking-decline, deaths-of-despair (area 02), urbanicity-and-depression

### Loneliness physiology: Cacioppo's evolutionary theory
- **id:** cacioppo-loneliness-physiology
- **type:** theory
- **disciplines:** social neuroscience; health psychology; psychoneuroimmunology
- **themes:** community-belonging, body-biology
- **layer:** relationships
- **proponents:** John T. Cacioppo, Stephanie Cacioppo, Louise Hawkley, Steven Cole
- **core claim:** Loneliness is perceived social isolation, a signal that evolved like hunger or pain to push people back towards others. Chronic loneliness triggers implicit hypervigilance for social threat. That vigilance biases attention and memory, disturbs sleep, and shifts immune gene expression towards inflammation. The theory gives a bodily route from social disconnection to depression and ill health.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Cacioppo, Cacioppo & Boomsma set out an evolutionary theory of loneliness. They review evidence on its heritability and argue it served adaptive functions in specific habitats [S49].
  - Hawkley & Cacioppo review a "loneliness regulatory loop", in which hypervigilance for social threat alters cognition, diminishes sleep quality and raises morbidity and mortality [S50].
  - Cole et al. compared 14 high- and low-lonely individuals. In high-lonely people's leukocytes, genes bearing anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid response elements were under-expressed and genes with pro-inflammatory NF-κB response elements were over-expressed [S51]. This is the founding observation of the "conserved transcriptional response to adversity" line of work.
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - The gene-expression evidence began with a very small sample (n = 14) [S51].
  - Smith et al.'s meta-analysis found *no* association between loneliness and CRP or fibrinogen. Loneliness was associated with IL-6 only in most-adjusted models, and objective isolation was associated with CRP and fibrinogen. They report mixed results, variable quality and methodological heterogeneity [S52].
  - The evolutionary account is plausible but hard to test directly. Arguments about adaptive function rest mainly on theory plus heritability and cross-species data [S49].
  - Loneliness and depression overlap heavily in measurement and may share causes, so separating "loneliness causes depression" from "depression causes loneliness" is difficult.
- **strength:** THEORETICAL. A coherent framework with some supporting data, but its inflammatory pathway is only weakly supported in meta-analysis.
- **related:** social-isolation-mortality, old-friends-inflammation, loneliness-trends (area 02), loneliness-disclosure (area 11), evolutionary-mismatch (area 05)
- **quote (optional):** "Loneliness is not simply being alone." — Hawkley & Cacioppo 2010, *Ann Behav Med*, abstract [S50]

### Social isolation, loneliness and mortality (and the "15 cigarettes" framing)
- **id:** social-isolation-mortality
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** epidemiology; health psychology
- **themes:** community-belonging, body-biology
- **layer:** relationships
- **proponents:** Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy Smith
- **core claim:** Weak social relationships predict earlier death about as strongly as established risk factors such as smoking and alcohol. This has been popularised as "loneliness is as bad as smoking 15 cigarettes a day." For this project the finding matters as evidence that social disconnection is biologically consequential, but the popular framing outruns the data.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton (2010) analysed 148 prospective studies (308,849 participants; average follow-up 7.5 years). People with stronger social relationships had 50% greater odds of survival (OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.42–1.59). The effect was largest for complex measures of social integration (OR 1.91) [S53].
  - Holt-Lunstad et al. (2015) estimated adjusted odds of mortality of 1.29 for social isolation, 1.26 for loneliness and 1.32 for living alone. Objective and subjective isolation did not differ [S54].
  - The 2010 paper's Figure 6 compares the social-relationship effect with effects from other meta-analyses, including one on smoking, and the authors conclude the influence is "comparable" to established risk factors [S53].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - The "15 cigarettes a day" line appears to derive from that Figure 6 comparison of effect sizes from *different* meta-analyses with different designs and outcomes. Its exact label could not be read in the text version, and its later use in public-health messaging was not verified here [S53]. The comparison uses the *largest* composite effect, for multidimensional social integration. Loneliness alone gives an OR of 1.26, about half the excess risk of the 1.50 headline [S54].
  - Initial health status influenced the 2015 results [S54]. Illness causes isolation as well as resulting from it, which is the classic reverse-causation problem.
  - The authors themselves acknowledge that causality "is not easily established" because isolation cannot be randomised [S53]. The meta-analysis pools observational studies, so residual confounding by socioeconomic status, depression and health behaviour is plausible.
  - No named critic's published critique of the "15 cigarettes" comparison was located and confirmed for this file.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. The prospective association between weak social ties and mortality is replicated across 148+ studies, but its causal size and the smoking comparison are contested.
- **related:** cacioppo-loneliness-physiology, loneliness-trends (area 02), loneliness-disclosure (area 11)
- **quote (optional):** "The influence of social relationships on risk for mortality is comparable with well-established risk factors for mortality." — Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010, *PLoS Med*, abstract [S53]

### Obesity and depression: bidirectional links and MR
- **id:** obesity-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** psychiatric epidemiology; genetic epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, stigma-silence, status-comparison
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Floriana Luppino, Brenda Penninx; Jessica Tyrrell, Timothy Frayling
- **core claim:** Obesity has risen across most countries since 1990 and is linked with depression in both directions. MR evidence suggests higher BMI partly *causes* depression, especially in women. This holds even for gene variants that raise BMI without its adverse metabolic consequences, which points to a psychological or social route such as stigma or body image, alongside a metabolic one.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Luppino et al. meta-analysed 15 longitudinal studies (N = 58,745). Baseline obesity raised the odds of later depression (OR 1.55) and overweight did too (OR 1.27). Baseline depression raised the odds of later obesity (OR 1.58) [S55].
  - Tyrrell et al. ran an MR study in UK Biobank (48,791 depression cases, 291,995 controls). One SD higher genetically determined BMI (4.9 kg/m²) gave OR 1.18 for depression in all participants and 1.24 in women. Results held for a "favourable adiposity" instrument without adverse metabolic effects, and meta-analysis with Psychiatric Genomics Consortium data strengthened the findings [S56].
  - NCD-RisC data show the combined prevalence of underweight and obesity increased in most countries between 1990 and 2022, with obesity predominating in most [S57]. So the exposure *has* risen.
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Luppino et al. found the obesity→depression link more pronounced in Americans and for clinical depression, and absent for overweight in under-20s [S55]. That pattern fits a cultural or stigma component at least as well as a purely biological one.
  - MR assumes the BMI variants affect depression only through BMI, the no-pleiotropy assumption. Tyrrell et al. report similar estimates across MR methods, though not all reached significance [S56].
  - An MR OR of 1.18 per 4.9 kg/m² is modest. Rising BMI could explain only a small part of any rise in distress.
- **strength:** WELL-SUPPORTED. Bidirectional prospective associations plus MR support a causal BMI→depression effect, though it is small.
- **related:** ultra-processed-food-depression, exercise-and-depression, old-friends-inflammation, stigma (area 11)

### Inflammation as a common pathway, and the "Old Friends" hypothesis
- **id:** old-friends-inflammation
- **type:** theory
- **disciplines:** psychoneuroimmunology; evolutionary medicine
- **themes:** body-biology, future-ecology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Graham Rook, Christopher Lowry, Charles Raison; Golam Khandaker (inflammation epidemiology)
- **core claim:** Many lifestyle factors here plausibly converge on low-grade systemic inflammation: obesity, inactivity, poor sleep, processed diets, air pollution and loneliness. The Old Friends hypothesis adds an evolutionary layer. Modern urban life has reduced exposure to the microorganisms that evolved with humans and trained immunoregulation. Without them, the same psychosocial stress produces more inflammation and so more depressive symptoms, especially in urban high-income settings.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Osimo et al. meta-analysed 37 studies (13,541 depressed patients, 155,728 controls). About 27% of depressed patients had low-grade inflammation (CRP > 3 mg/L), and the odds of low-grade inflammation versus matched controls were 1.46 [S58]. Mechanistic detail is covered in area 03.
  - Rook, Lowry & Raison argue that chronic inflammatory and psychiatric conditions are both more common in urban than rural populations, and in migrants from low- to high-income countries who move in infancy. They suggest diminished perinatal exposure to "Old Friends" reduces stress resilience [S59].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Most depressed patients do *not* show low-grade inflammation: about 73% have CRP ≤ 3 mg/L [S58]. Inflammation could explain at most a subgroup.
  - Rook et al. present the Old Friends account as a concept that "suggests novel interpretations". It is built from converging epidemiological patterns and animal work rather than direct tests in human depression [S59].
  - The loneliness–inflammation link is weak in meta-analysis [S52], so the claimed convergence is uneven across exposures.
- **strength:** SPECULATIVE. Inflammation in a subgroup of depressed people is established, but Old Friends as an explanation of modern depression lacks direct human evidence.
- **related:** inflammatory-model-of-depression (area 03), gut-microbiome-depression, urbanicity-and-depression, obesity-depression, cacioppo-loneliness-physiology

### Gut microbiome and depression
- **id:** gut-microbiome-depression
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** microbiology; psychiatry
- **themes:** body-biology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Mireia Valles-Colomer and the Flemish Gut Flora Project team; critics including Jens Walter, B. Brett Finlay, Fergus Shanahan
- **core claim:** The gut–brain axis is a fashionable explanation for diet- and lifestyle-related mood problems. The best population evidence links specific gut bacteria to quality of life and depression, but causality in humans remains unshown, and animal "transfer" studies have overstated it.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Valles-Colomer et al. studied the Flemish Gut Flora Project (n = 1,054), validated in independent datasets (n = 1,070). Butyrate-producing *Faecalibacterium* and *Coprococcus* were associated with higher quality of life. *Coprococcus* and *Dialister* were depleted in depression even after correcting for antidepressant use. Microbial capacity to synthesise a dopamine metabolite correlated with mental quality of life [S60].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Valles-Colomer et al. is cross-sectional, and the authors explicitly emphasise "confounder importance" [S60]. Diet, medication, BMI and bowel habits all affect both the microbiome and mood.
  - Walter et al. systematically reviewed studies that transplant human microbiota into rodents. 95% (36/38) reported transferring disease phenotypes, which they argue is "implausible and overstates the role of the gut microbiome" [S61].
  - No large human RCT evidence for microbiome-targeted depression *prevention* was verified for this file.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Replicated associations exist in human cohorts, but they are cross-sectional and confounded, and the causal evidence comes mainly from animal models that critics consider inflated.
- **related:** old-friends-inflammation, ultra-processed-food-depression, diet-intervention-trials
- **quote (optional):** "this exceedingly high rate of inter-species transferable pathologies is implausible and overstates the role of the gut microbiome" — Walter et al. 2020, *Cell*, abstract [S61]

### Environmental toxins: air pollution, lead (and microplastics)
- **id:** air-pollution-lead-toxins
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** environmental epidemiology; developmental psychopathology
- **themes:** body-biology, future-ecology, institutions-power
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Isobel Braithwaite, Joseph Hayes; Ioannis Bakolis, Ian Mudway; Aaron Reuben, Terrie Moffitt, Avshalom Caspi
- **core claim:** Long-term exposure to fine particulate and traffic-related air pollution is associated with higher risk of depression and common mental disorders. Childhood lead exposure is associated with modestly higher adult psychopathology and less favourable personality traits. Both are structurally distributed exposures that individuals cannot easily choose.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Braithwaite et al. found, for long-term PM2.5 exposure and depression, a pooled OR of 1.102 per 10 μg/m³ (95% CI 1.023–1.189; 5 studies). They also found an association between short-term PM10 exposure and suicide [S62].
  - Bakolis et al. followed 1,698 adults in South East London from 2008 to 2013, adjusting for socioeconomic position and road-traffic noise. Interquartile-range increases in PM2.5, NOx and NO2 were associated with 18–39% higher odds of common mental disorders, with stronger associations among people who did not move house [S63].
  - Reuben et al. used the Dunedin birth cohort (born 1972–73), with blood lead measured at age 11 in 579 members. Each 5 μg/dL increase was associated with a 1.34-point increase in general psychopathology on a scale with mean 100 and SD 15, about 0.09 SD, driven by internalising and thought-disorder symptoms. It was also associated with higher neuroticism and lower agreeableness and conscientiousness [S64].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Braithwaite et al. themselves cite "the limited literature", heterogeneous outcome definitions and exposure assessment, and residual confounding [S62]. Only 5 studies fed the depression estimate.
  - Bakolis et al. note that "causation cannot be proved" [S63].
  - The lead effect is small, and lead exposure in wealthy countries is far *lower* in recent cohorts than in the 1970s, as Reuben et al.'s framing of "millions of adults now entering middle age" implies [S64]. So lead cannot explain rising distress among today's young people, though it could have shaped older cohorts.
  - The direction of air-pollution trends in many wealthy cities, which have often improved since the late twentieth century, would also cut against pollution as a driver of *rising* distress. No air-quality time series was verified for this file, so this point is UNVERIFIED.
  - **Microplastics:** no human evidence linking microplastic exposure to depression or anxiety was located. This topic is not established and is mentioned only so readers know it was considered. A search for such evidence was not exhaustive.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Air pollution associations are consistent but few, small and confounding-prone. The lead effects come from one cohort, are small, and point the wrong way for recent trends.
- **related:** urbanicity-and-depression, noise-heat, old-friends-inflammation, climate-anxiety (area 09)

### Earlier puberty and adolescent psychopathology
- **id:** pubertal-timing
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** paediatric endocrinology; developmental psychopathology
- **themes:** body-biology, self-identity, status-comparison
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Christian Eckert-Lind, Anders Juul (secular trend); Josie Ullsperger, Molly Nikolas (meta-analysis)
- **core claim:** Puberty is starting earlier, and youths who mature earlier than their peers show more psychopathology. If earlier puberty means more years of adolescent vulnerability, or more mismatch between physical and social maturity, it could be one bodily contributor to rising adolescent distress, especially among girls.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Eckert-Lind et al. meta-analysed 30 studies of clinically assessed breast development. Age at thelarche fell by 0.24 years (almost 3 months) per decade from 1977 to 2013, with variation by geography and ethnicity [S65].
  - Ullsperger & Nikolas meta-analysed 101 studies. Early pubertal timing was associated with psychopathology across internalising and externalising domains, with small effects (d ≈ 0.20) [S66].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Contrary to the common assumption that early puberty is a specifically female risk, Ullsperger & Nikolas found that sex did *not* moderate the early-timing effect, which appeared in both boys and girls [S66].
  - Pubertal *timing relative to peers*, the predictor in most studies, is different from a population-wide *shift* in pubertal age. If everyone matures earlier, relative earliness does not change. Whether a secular shift raises population distress has not been directly tested in the sources reviewed.
  - The decline in pubertal age is gradual across decades [S65]. It does not obviously match the sharper post-2010 rises in adolescent distress reported in some countries (see area 04).
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Both the trend and the early-timing association are documented, but the link from a population-wide shift to rising distress is untested.
- **related:** obesity-depression, hormonal-contraception-depression, adolescent-mental-health-trends (area 04), gender-differences-depression (area 04)

### Hormonal contraception and depression
- **id:** hormonal-contraception-depression
- **type:** debate
- **disciplines:** pharmacoepidemiology; gynaecology; psychiatry
- **themes:** body-biology, measurement-diagnosis
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Charlotte Wessel Skovlund, Øjvind Lidegaard (Danish registry); Therese Johansson, Åsa Johansson (UK Biobank); Niklas Zethraeus, Angelica Lindén Hirschberg (RCT)
- **core claim:** Hormonal contraception is used by very large numbers of women and adolescent girls in wealthy countries. Registry and cohort data associate starting it, especially in adolescence and in the first months or years, with higher rates of antidepressant use and depression diagnosis. A placebo-controlled RCT found lower general wellbeing but no significant effect on depressive symptoms.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Skovlund et al. followed 1,061,997 Danish women aged 15–34 from 2000 to 2013. Compared with non-users, combined oral contraceptive users had an RR of 1.23 for first antidepressant use, progestogen-only pill users 1.34, patch users 2.0, vaginal ring users 1.6 and levonorgestrel IUS users 1.4. Among adolescents aged 15–19, the RR was 1.8 for combined pills and 2.2 for progestogen-only pills, and risk peaked about 6 months after starting [S67].
  - Johansson et al. studied 264,557 UK Biobank women. The first 2 years of oral contraceptive use were associated with HR 1.71 for depression versus never-users. Adolescent use was associated with higher later risk, and a sibling-comparison analysis supported a causal interpretation. The authors argue that "healthy user bias", where women who react badly discontinue, has hidden effects in earlier studies [S69].
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Zethraeus et al. ran a double-blind RCT in 340 healthy Swedish women aged 18–35, comparing a levonorgestrel combined pill with placebo for 3 months. The pill lowered general wellbeing (PGWBI −4.12) but had no statistically significant effect on depressive symptoms (BDI) [S68].
  - Antidepressant prescription is a proxy for depression and is influenced by contact with doctors. Women starting contraception see clinicians more often, which may raise detection (surveillance bias). This is a standard critique of registry designs.
  - Absolute risk increases are small even where relative risks are raised. Skovlund et al.'s relative risks fall with age [S67].
  - The trend logic is unclear. Contraceptive use is not new in wealthy countries, and its timing does not obviously match recent rises in distress.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. Large cohorts show associations, strongest in adolescents and recent starters, but the one RCT found effects on wellbeing rather than depression, and surveillance and confounding biases are plausible.
- **related:** pubertal-timing, gender-differences-depression (area 04), diagnostic-expansion (area 04)

### Noise and heat (brief)
- **id:** noise-heat
- **type:** empirical-finding
- **disciplines:** environmental epidemiology
- **themes:** body-biology, future-ecology
- **layer:** body
- **proponents:** Janice Hegewald, Andreas Seidler (noise); Rhiannon Thompson (heat review)
- **core claim:** Chronic environmental noise and high ambient temperatures are additional physical stressors. Aircraft noise is associated with modestly higher depression risk, and hot weather with higher suicide risk.
- **evidence / arguments for:**
  - Hegewald et al. meta-analysed 31 studies. Depression risk rose by 12% per 10 dB L_DEN of aircraft noise (ES 1.12, 95% CI 1.02–1.23; 5 studies) [S70].
  - Thompson et al. reviewed 35 studies. The strongest evidence was for increased suicide risk with heat, with 15 studies reporting RR 1.014–1.37 per 1 °C [S71]. Area 09 covers this.
- **evidence / arguments against, and critics:**
  - Road traffic (11 studies) and railway noise (3 studies) showed only 2–3% non-significant increases in depression risk per 10 dB [S70].
  - Thompson et al.'s included studies were heterogeneous and observational, which made meta-analysis infeasible [S71].
  - Neither exposure has a clear trend that maps onto rising chronic depression or anxiety. Heat matters more for acute events such as suicide and admissions.
- **strength:** CONTESTED. A modest aircraft-noise association and a more consistent heat–suicide association, both observational and narrow in scope.
- **related:** air-pollution-lead-toxins, urbanicity-and-depression, heat-and-suicide (area 09)

## Steelman of the rival / deflationary view(s)

**1. The "boring proximal causes" steelman (against cultural theory).** Suppose one knew nothing about neoliberalism, disenchantment or social media. One would still find many people in wealthy societies who sleep badly, rarely move, sit indoors under dim light and bright evenings, eat diets dominated by ultra-processed foods, carry more weight, and live near traffic. The trial evidence that changing some of these changes mood is real. Treating insomnia gives moderate reductions in depressive symptoms (g ≈ −0.63 across 65 RCTs [S9]; d = 0.48 for PHQ-9 in OASIS [S8]). Exercise gives moderate reductions in RCTs [S18], and activity is protective in cohorts and in MR [S15, S17]. MR supports a causal effect of BMI on depression [S56]. Bright light treats SAD [S26]. On this view, much of the "unexplained" quiet misery could be the summed effect of many small physiological insults, and grand cultural explanations are unnecessary for a large share of the variance. This is roughly how a lifestyle-medicine or biomedical clinician might see it, and it fits the area 03 view that depression has multiple proximal mechanisms.

**2. The deflationary steelman against this area itself.** The opposite argument is equally strong. Most of these factors fail the *timing* test:
- Objectively measured adult sleep shows no secular decline [S6], and time-use data show short sleep falling in the US and UK [S5].
- Youth drinking has *fallen* [S43, S44].
- Global insufficient activity was flat from 2001 to 2016 [S13].
- Lead exposure is far lower in recent cohorts [S64].
- Air quality in many wealthy cities has plausibly improved (UNVERIFIED here).
- Pubertal age has drifted slowly over decades [S65], not in a sharp break.

Effects are mostly small: an OR of 1.10 per 10 μg/m³ PM2.5 [S62], a 0.09-SD rise in psychopathology per 5 μg/dL of lead [S64], and a β of −0.06 for digital engagement and sleep [S12]. Much of the observational evidence is exposed to reverse causation, because depression itself causes poor sleep, inactivity, comfort eating, weight gain, staying indoors and social withdrawal. Where blinding is impossible, expectancy inflates trial results, as the Cochrane exercise review shows: the effect shrinks from SMD −0.62 to a non-significant −0.18 in the best-designed trials [S19], and SMILES has been criticised on the same grounds [S36]. The best nutritional prevention trials were null or slightly adverse [S40, S42]. On this view, lifestyle factors are real modifiers of individual risk but cannot explain why distress has risen.

**3. The "downstream of social organisation" steelman.** A third view, associated with social epidemiology and taken up in area 02, accepts the mechanisms but denies that they are independent causes. Indoor sedentary work, car-dependent suburbs, long hours, shift work, cheap ultra-processed food, housing next to main roads, scarce green space in poor neighbourhoods, and loneliness built into how people live are features of economic and urban organisation, not personal choices. The urban–psychosis gradient [S30], the green-space gradient [S28] and the pollution gradient [S63] all track place and class. On this reading, "body and lifestyle" are the *route* by which social structure gets under the skin, not a rival to social explanations.

**4. The critique that lifestyle framing individualises responsibility.** A related critique, developed in area 11 (Fisher's "privatisation of stress" and similar), holds that telling depressed people to sleep, exercise and eat better quietly moves responsibility from institutions to individuals. When those interventions fail or cannot be sustained, it can add self-blame. It can also crowd out collective remedies such as clean-air rules, working-time regulation, urban green space and food policy. This critique does not deny the trial evidence. It concerns what kind of action the evidence is taken to imply.

## Internal tensions

- **Sleep trends: decline or myth?** Keyes et al. find a clear decline in US adolescents [S1], and Matricciani et al.'s pooled analysis finds more than an hour lost over a century [S3]. Yet Matricciani's own earlier review called the claim's scientific basis "limited" [S2], and adult studies find no objective decline [S6] and mixed time-use trends [S5]. The literature disagrees by age group, by country and by measurement method.
- **Screens and sleep.** Systematic reviews of cross-sectional studies report consistent and sometimes large associations (OR ≈ 2 [S11]; 90% of studies [S10]). Time-use diary analysis finds small associations and no substantive effect of pre-bed technology use on sleep amount [S12]. The disagreement is largely about measurement.
- **Diet as treatment versus prevention.** Nutritional psychiatry's flagship trial reports d ≈ 1.16 [S35], while the largest prevention RCTs find nothing [S40] or slight harm [S42]. Proponents emphasise whole-diet change over supplements and treatment over prevention. Critics emphasise blinding and expectancy [S36].
- **Exercise: strong or weak?** Network meta-analysis supports exercise as a core depression treatment with moderate effects [S18]. The Cochrane review finds the effect disappears in methodologically rigorous trials [S19]. Both cite largely overlapping literatures but weight trial quality differently.
- **Loneliness and inflammation.** Cacioppo's school proposes a pro-inflammatory pathway [S50, S51], but meta-analysis finds no loneliness–CRP link and only a fragile loneliness–IL-6 link [S52].
- **Early puberty and girls.** Clinical and popular accounts often treat early puberty as a specifically female risk. The largest meta-analysis finds similar effects in boys [S66].
- **Holt-Lunstad's headline versus specific measures.** The headline OR of 1.50 and the even larger 1.91 for complex integration [S53] drive the "as bad as smoking" message. Loneliness specifically gives OR 1.26 [S54].

## Cross-area tensions

- **Versus area 06 (technology).** Haidt-style accounts often route harm through lost sleep. This area's best-measured evidence suggests modest technology–sleep associations [S12], and the adolescent sleep decline began before smartphones [S1]. Technology critics might reply that smartphones intensified an existing trend. Sedentary screen time is associated with depression more than active time [S20, S21], which fits displacement accounts, the idea that screen time crowds out activity.
- **Versus area 02 (sociology, deaths of despair).** Falling youth drinking [S43] complicates any story in which young people increasingly self-medicate with alcohol. Opioid deaths, a different and older demographic, belong to area 02. Lifestyle mechanisms may mediate social-structural causes, which area 02 would emphasise, rather than compete with them.
- **Versus area 03 (psychiatry).** Inflammation, MR, and treatment effects of exercise and CBT-I are shared ground. The tension is about emphasis. Biomedical psychiatry treats these as individual-level targets, while this area's social reading treats them as population exposures.
- **Versus area 04 (measurement).** Several exposures here are measured through proxies that are themselves shaped by help-seeking, such as antidepressant prescriptions in the contraception studies [S67] or registry diagnoses in the green-space study [S28]. Rising diagnosis or prescribing could inflate apparent effects or trends.
- **Versus area 05 (evolutionary mismatch).** Indoor life [S22], light patterns [S23, S24], lost "Old Friends" [S59] and loneliness as an evolved alarm [S49] are mismatch arguments with bodily mechanisms. This area's evidence is a check on mismatch romanticism. Mismatch claims are strongest where there are trials (light for SAD, exercise) and weakest where they rest on speculation (Old Friends).
- **Versus area 01 (critical and cultural theory).** Cultural theorists who see depression as a meaning or alienation problem face the sleep and exercise trial evidence, in which non-cultural interventions move symptoms. Conversely, critical theorists can read lifestyle prescriptions as a form of the individualisation they critique (see area 11).
- **Versus area 09 (ecology).** Heat–suicide evidence [S71] is a bodily channel for climate effects on mental health, distinct from climate *anxiety*.

## Open questions / what this area cannot explain

- **Timing.** Which, if any, bodily or environmental exposure worsened in wealthy countries in the specific years and groups where distress rose, for example adolescent girls after about 2010 in some countries? Most candidates here are flat, improving or changing slowly.
- **Aggregation.** Could many small individual-level effects (sleep, activity, light, diet, weight) add up to a meaningful population shift even if none alone fits the trend? No study reviewed here models their joint contribution to secular change.
- **Reverse causation at scale.** How much of the observational evidence on diet, light, activity, green space and loneliness reflects depression changing behaviour, rather than the reverse? Lag analyses and MR help but are available only for some exposures.
- **Blinding.** Can lifestyle trials ever be adequately controlled for expectancy? If not, how should their effect sizes be weighed against drug trials?
- **Measurement.** Self-reported sleep, activity and outdoor time may drift over time as norms change. Are some "trends" artefacts of how people describe their days?
- **Mediation versus independent cause.** Are these bodily factors independent contributors, or mainly channels through which social and economic organisation affects mood? Most studies adjust for socioeconomic status, which may remove the very pathway of interest.
- **Subgroups.** Inflammation is present in about a quarter of depressed patients [S58], and light sensitivity varies genetically. Do bodily explanations apply strongly to some people and not at all to others, and is that why population-level stories seem to fail?
- **"Numbness" and quiet misery.** Almost all evidence here concerns diagnosed depression, symptom scales and anxiety. Very little addresses diffuse low mood, anhedonia without disorder, or "languishing", the quieter states in the project's question.
- **Absent evidence.** For some popular candidates, such as microplastics, no mental-health evidence was found. It remains open whether this is genuine absence or simply unstudied.

## Thinkers (for the who's who)

- **John T. Cacioppo (1951–2018)**, social neuroscience. Key works: *Loneliness: Human Nature and the Need for Social Connection* (with William Patrick, 2008); "Evolutionary mechanisms for loneliness" (2014) [S49]; "Loneliness matters" (with Hawkley, 2010) [S50]. Key ideas: loneliness is perceived social isolation, an evolved signal like hunger, and when chronic it produces social-threat hypervigilance, poor sleep and pro-inflammatory gene expression. Key critics: meta-analysts of loneliness and inflammation such as Smith et al. find weak or inconsistent biomarker links [S52]; the early transcriptomic work used very small samples [S51].
- **Julianne Holt-Lunstad (contemporary)**, health psychology. Key works: meta-analyses of social relationships and mortality (2010, 2015) [S53, S54]. Key ideas: social disconnection predicts mortality comparably to established risk factors. Key critics: the observational design and reverse causation from ill health; the popular smoking comparison relies on composite measures and cross-meta-analysis comparisons (no named critique verified here).
- **Felice Jacka (contemporary)**, nutritional psychiatry. Key works: SMILES trial (2017) [S35]; co-author of the UPF umbrella review [S33]. Key ideas: diet quality is a modifiable risk factor and treatment target for depression. Key critics: Molendijk, Fried & Van der Does on expectancy, blinding and recruitment in SMILES [S36]; large null prevention trials such as MoodFOOD [S40].
- **Daniel Freeman (contemporary)**, clinical psychology. Key works: OASIS trial (2017) [S8]. Key ideas: insomnia is a causal contributor to psychotic experiences and other mental-health problems, testable by treating sleep. Key critics: high and differential attrition, unblinded self-report outcomes, student sample (limitations noted in the paper itself).
- **Katherine Keyes (contemporary)**, psychiatric epidemiology. Key works: "The great sleep recession" (2015) [S1]. Key ideas: US adolescent self-reported sleep declined from the 1990s, with period effects. Key critics: sleep-trend sceptics such as Matricciani et al. [S2] and Youngstedt et al. [S6], who question the generality and measurement of decline claims.
- **Lisa Matricciani and Tim Olds (contemporary)**, sleep and population health. Key works: 2011 review of the claim of declining child sleep [S2]; 2012 century-long pooled analysis [S3]. Key ideas: the popular claim of declining sleep rests on a thin citation trail, yet pooled data do show declines in many regions. Their work is itself internally nuanced.
- **Gregory Bratman (contemporary)**, environmental psychology. Key works: the 2015 PNAS nature-walk study [S27]; the 2019 ecosystem-services consensus [S29]. Key ideas: nature experience reduces rumination and supports mental health, with implications for urban planning. Key critics: small samples, short-term outcomes and confounding in green-space epidemiology (a general critique; no single named critic verified).
- **Graham Rook (contemporary)**, medical microbiology and immunology. Key works: "Microbial 'Old Friends', immunoregulation and stress resilience" (with Lowry and Raison, 2013) [S59]. Key ideas: loss of co-evolved microbial exposures impairs immunoregulation and stress resilience in urban modernity. Key critics: the evidence in human depression is indirect, and microbiome causality claims are often overstated [S61].
- **Marta Di Forti and Robin Murray (contemporary)**, psychiatry. Key works: EU-GEI cannabis study (2019) [S47]. Key ideas: daily high-potency cannabis substantially raises psychosis risk and helps explain geographic variation in incidence. Key critics: case-control design and the assumptions behind attributable-fraction estimates.
- **Sean Cain and Angus Burns (contemporary)**, chronobiology. Key works: UK Biobank daylight and night-light studies (2021, 2022/23) [S23, S24]. Key ideas: low daytime and bright night-time light are environmental risk factors for psychiatric disorders. Key critics: cross-sectional and observational design and reverse causation (limitations acknowledged by the authors).

## Glossary

- **Mendelian randomisation (MR):** using genetic variants that influence an exposure (e.g., BMI, activity) as natural experiments to estimate causal effects on an outcome. It assumes the variants affect the outcome only via the exposure (no pleiotropy).
- **CBT-I:** cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia. A structured psychological treatment (sleep restriction, stimulus control, cognitive work), here sometimes delivered digitally.
- **Age-period-cohort analysis:** a statistical method that separates changes due to age, calendar time (period) and birth cohort.
- **Ultra-processed food (UPF):** NOVA group 4. Industrial formulations made largely from substances extracted or derived from foods, plus additives.
- **NOVA:** a food classification system that groups foods by the degree and purpose of processing.
- **Thelarche:** onset of breast development (Tanner stage 2), used as a marker of pubertal onset in girls.
- **Pubertal timing:** maturing early or late *relative to peers*, distinct from the population's average pubertal age.
- **Low-grade inflammation:** mildly elevated systemic inflammatory markers, e.g., CRP > 3 mg/L, without acute infection.
- **Old Friends hypothesis:** the idea that reduced exposure to co-evolved microbes impairs immune regulation, raising inflammatory and possibly psychiatric risk. An extension of the hygiene hypothesis.
- **Seasonal affective disorder (SAD):** recurrent depression with a seasonal (usually winter) pattern.
- **Light at night (LAN):** exposure to artificial light during the biological night, which may disrupt circadian rhythms.
- **NHAPS:** the US National Human Activity Pattern Survey (1992–94), a source of time-in-environment estimates.
- **Population attributable fraction (PAF):** the proportion of cases that would be prevented if an exposure were removed, assuming causality.
- **Healthy user bias:** in medication studies, distortion that arises because people who tolerate a drug keep using it while those harmed stop, making long-term users look healthier.
- **Expectancy effect:** improvement due to participants' expectations of benefit, especially hard to rule out in unblinded lifestyle trials.
- **Human microbiota-associated (HMA) rodents:** germ-free animals colonised with human gut microbes to test causal effects.
